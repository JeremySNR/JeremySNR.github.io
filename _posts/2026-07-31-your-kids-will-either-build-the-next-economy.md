*AI Innovation Partner @ Travel Counsellors*

Somewhere right now, a child is being born who will spend their working life inside an economy that's already being decided. Not by them. Not even really by their parents. By a handful of decisions being made, or not made, in the next few years, about who owns the infrastructure their entire working life will run on.

That's not a metaphor. It's a literal, specific choice, and Britain is currently choosing the wrong side of it by default, by saying nothing at all.

Today, Andy Burnham stood on the steps of Downing Street and gave his first speech as Prime Minister. He talked about "reindustrialising Britain, using public procurement to back British industry." He talked about building "a new economy" where "life's essentials" come back "under stronger public control." He talked about taking power out of Westminster "into every postcode in the land," and about a ten-year plan, due later this year, laying out where he wants the country to be.

He did not say the words AI or artificial intelligence once. Not in a speech that used the word "reindustrialising" out loud, on the one day it might have actually meant something. Not in a speech aimed at exactly the generation who'll live with the consequences of that omission for the next fifty years.

Within hours of being sworn in, his first call was reportedly to Donald Trump. Make of that what you will. It's a fairly precise image of where Britain currently sits: dependent on America for the relationship that matters most in the room, with no plan of our own for the technology that relationship increasingly runs through.

To be fair to him, this isn't quite a blank slate. Three weeks earlier, in Manchester, he said Britain could be "the world's leading innovation nation" and pledged to "back our scientists, technologists, entrepreneurs and creatives." Fine words. But there's a difference between naming an ambition and having a plan, and on the technology that will decide whether "reindustrialising Britain" means anything at all, he has offered nothing. Not a compute target. Not a number. Not a plan. Just the word "innovation," doing a lot of work on its own.

Here's what that gap actually means for a child born this year. By the time they're choosing a career, the companies worth working for, the industries paying the best wages, the places generating the tax base that funds their schools and hospitals, will very likely be built on AI infrastructure owned by someone else's country. Not because that was inevitable. Because we didn't build our own while we had the chance, and by the time it was obvious, it was too late to catch up cheaply.

That's the actual stakes here. Not a spreadsheet. A generation who either owns a piece of the next economy, or spends their working life renting access to one built somewhere else, on someone else's terms, cancellable at someone else's convenience.

## The number that should have been in that speech

Here it is. In 2025, global AI venture capital hit $258.7 billion. That's according to the OECD, published in February this year. Of that money, the US took 75%, or $194 billion. The UK took 5%, or $13.8 billion. We came fourth, just ahead of China's 5%, essentially tied with the EU's 6%.

Read that again. We are level with a stagnant continental bloc and a country under chip sanctions. That's not "behind." That's a rounding error next to America. And every year that rounding error persists is a year closer to that child leaving school into an economy that's already been built by somebody else.

Now look at what governments, not just markets, are putting behind this. The US is backing Stargate, the OpenAI-SoftBank-Oracle-Nvidia project, to the tune of $500 billion and 10 gigawatts of compute by 2029. By late last year it already had roughly 7 gigawatts planned and $400 billion committed. The EU has put €200 billion behind InvestAI, including a ring-fenced €20 billion for AI "gigafactories," and had 76 project bids worth over €230 billion by the time the call closed. China has a five-year, $295 billion state AI plan and has banned foreign chips from its state-funded data centres outright, backing its own hardware even though Huawei's Ascend chips still run at roughly 6% of the performance of Nvidia's best. Abu Dhabi's sovereign fund MGX closed a single $49 billion AI investment vehicle on the 1st of July, above target, aiming for $100 billion under management. Saudi Arabia has signed $23 billion in AI infrastructure deals in the last year alone, chasing a target of AI contributing 12% of GDP by 2030. The UAE's target for the same year is 40%.

And the UK. Our sovereign AI compute plan, the AI Research Resource, went from 2 to 21 AI ExaFLOPs last year, a genuine tenfold increase, with a target of 420 by 2030. The Sovereign AI Unit, launched in April, is £500 million, run like a venture fund inside government, backing companies like Callosum with equity and compute time. Total government commitment to sovereign AI, across every scheme going, comes to around £3.5 billion.

Three and a half billion pounds, against half a trillion dollars from the Americans alone. We're not slightly behind. We are playing an entirely different game, at an entirely different scale, and calling it the same competition.

## Why this isn't abstract

This isn't a theoretical worry about league tables. In June this year, the US government sent Anthropic a letter at twenty-one minutes past five on a Friday afternoon, citing national security, and ordered the company to cut off access to its two most capable models, Fable 5 and Mythos 5, for every non-American on the planet. No warning. No consultation. No exemption for the UK. Anthropic itself pushed back publicly, saying the government's own standard, if applied consistently, "would essentially halt all new model deployments for all frontier model providers." It didn't matter. The models went dark for three weeks before access was restored on the 1st of July.

I've written about this before, so I won't go over the whole saga again. But the point that matters for Burnham specifically is this: whatever workflows, products or advantage British businesses and government departments had built on those models simply stopped working, by decision of a foreign government, with zero say from anyone in London. That is what dependency actually looks like when it stops being a policy paper and starts being a Tuesday morning where your tools don't work.

Chatham House said it plainly the day Burnham took over: "The UK's dependencies on US technology run extremely deep... a strategy resting on proximity to the US alone looks less sensible with every passing day." The Tony Blair Institute has gone further, warning that if the UK doesn't build meaningfully more compute by 2030, it risks holding "less than 1 per cent of the world's total AI compute," down from a best case of around 3%, against America's roughly 75%.

To their credit, this seems to be exactly what's rattling Burnham's own team. Reporting from the Financial Times suggests his advisers, including former tech minister Josh Simons, think the current approach, close alignment with US tech giants under Peter Mandelson's old playbook, has been "a geopolitical failure that hasn't delivered on its intended aims." One person involved called it "unfettered tech boosterism."

They're not wrong that the old approach hasn't delivered. Where I think they're at risk of getting it wrong is the conclusion. The answer to "we're too dependent on America" isn't to regulate our way out of the room. It's to build enough of our own capability that we're not just taking whatever access we're given. Right now the plan sounds like it's heavier on limiting foreign ownership of data centres and reviewing AI Growth Zones than it is on getting more compute built on British soil. That's control without capability, and control without capability is just a different flavour of dependency, one where we've made ourselves smaller instead of stronger.

## The part I'm not going to wave away

Here's where I have to be honest, because pretending the other side of this argument doesn't exist would make the whole piece worthless.

A report from Global Action Plan this April found that 84% of proposed UK data centre developments are in areas that are already water-stressed, or projected to be by 2040. Slough already has at least 32 data centres running, in a region the Environment Agency itself classifies as seriously water stressed, and Thames Water estimates that under a high-growth scenario, data centres could drive nearly 30% of all new water demand in the area, around 270 million litres a day. In Scotland, there are roughly 24 proposed hyperscale data centres, and if all of them go ahead, some estimates suggest they could consume up to one and a half times Scotland's peak electricity demand. Edinburgh refused a hyperscale site outright this February. The SNP and the Scottish Greens are now both backing a national moratorium.

These are not manufactured objections. Households and farmers are being asked to cut water use while an industry that runs on enormous quantities of it gets waved through planning. That tension is real, and anyone writing about this honestly has to sit with it rather than dismiss it as nimbyism.

But here's the thing I keep coming back to. The upside being talked about isn't small money for a small risk. A Google-commissioned study estimates AI could add more than £400 billion to the UK economy by 2030, the equivalent of an extra 2.6% of annual growth, enough on its own to end the growth stagnation we've been stuck in for years. PwC's own estimate has England's GDP up to 10.6% higher by the end of the decade because of AI. Set against that, £3.5 billion in sovereign compute investment doesn't look ambitious. It looks like we've priced in losing before we've even tried to win.

There's a version of this argument that's been made before, at a different hinge point in history, and it's worth being precise about it rather than just gesturing at it. The Industrial Revolution didn't make everyone richer overnight. Economic historians estimate British income per person barely moved from about $400 in 1760 to $430 in 1800, and didn't really take off until 1830 to 1860, when it jumped to $800. Real wages lagged behind productivity for decades, a gap economists still call Engels' Pause. The people objecting to mills and factories in 1780 weren't wrong that the changes were disruptive and unevenly felt. They were wrong that resisting it would stop it from happening anyway, and the countries that industrialised late but seriously, Germany, Japan, eventually the US itself, ended up overtaking Britain, the country that got there first but grew complacent.

The economist Alexander Gerschenkron studied exactly this pattern. Latecomers to an industrial transition can leapfrog the countries that went first, he argued, but only if they mobilise resources and state backing at a scale the pioneers never needed, precisely because they're playing catch-up. Britain doesn't get to be the pioneer in AI. That ship has sailed. What we can still be is a serious fast follower. But fast followers don't get there by being cautious. They get there by building hard and building fast, and dealing with the friction as it comes, not by treating the friction as a reason not to start.

## So where does that leave Andy Burnham

He stood outside Number 10 today and talked about reindustrialising Britain, about rebuilding this country's industrial base, about being the innovation nation of the next decade. He said all of that, today, in the speech where he actually took the job. The technology most likely to determine whether any of it happens didn't get a single mention.

I don't know if that's because his team genuinely hasn't worked out where they stand yet, or because they've concluded that regulating American tech giants is an easier story to tell than building British compute at scale. Both are plausible. Neither is good enough, and neither answers the number that actually matters here: 5% of global AI investment, level with a sanctioned economy, against America's 75%.

Every month that number doesn't move is a month closer to that child growing up into whatever we've built, or failed to build, in the meantime. Not as an abstraction. As their actual job market, their actual wages, their actual chance of working somewhere that owns what it builds rather than renting someone else's platform on someone else's terms. That was true yesterday. It's still true today, now that the speech has been given and the plan still hasn't.

Mr Burnham, you asked for the job of rebalancing power in this country. You said so yourself, today, on the steps of Downing Street. Here's the question that actually tests whether you meant it.

Are we going to build the economy the next generation grows up inside? Or are we going to keep renting one from somewhere else, and hope nobody notices the difference until it's too late to change it?
